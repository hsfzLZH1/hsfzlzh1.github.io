1. The question

Change one sign in the determinant of a two-by-two matrix and you get its permanent. The determinant is ad−bcad-bcad−bc. The permanent is ad+bcad+bcad+bc. Each costs two multiplications and one more operation. Now let the matrix grow. Gaussian elimination still gives an efficient way to compute the determinant. Can any sequence of arithmetic operations do the same for the permanent over the rational numbers?

The number of terms offers a tempting but unreliable answer. Both polynomials sum over all permutations of the columns. A ten-by-ten matrix already gives more than three million terms in each expansion. The determinant illustrates how much work one can save by computing the right intermediate quantities. Proving that the permanent is hard requires ruling out every way a circuit might reuse quantities or cancel terms, including ways nobody has thought of yet.

This is one route into Valiant’s question about VP and VNP. Family 379 claims a separation, a sharp exponent for matrix multiplication, faster Fourier transforms and integer products, and an exponential bound in a problem about intersecting sets. To see why these problems share a family number, consider a simpler question that runs through the papers. If many parts of a calculation need the same information, how much does it cost to make that information available to all of them?

2. Background and essential definitions

Fix a field FFF, such as the rationals. An arithmetic circuit starts with variables and field constants and builds a polynomial using gates for addition, subtraction and multiplication. Its size counts gates. A gate can feed many later gates. A formula, by contrast, has the shape of a tree. Writing the same subcalculation in two branches generally pays for it twice.

VP consists of families of polynomials. The member indexed by nnn has polynomially many variables, polynomially bounded degree and a circuit whose size is bounded by a polynomial in nnn. The circuit can be chosen separately for each nnn. VNP allows one further operation. Given a VP family gng_ngn​ and a polynomially bounded number p(n)p(n)p(n) of extra bits, form

fn(x)=∑b∈{0,1}p(n)gn(x,b).f_n(x)=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{p(n)}}g_n(x,b).fn​(x)=b∈{0,1}p(n)∑​gn​(x,b).

With the bits fixed, one summand is easy to compute. Must the whole sum have a small circuit? Sometimes it does. For example, the sum of the weights of all subsets of nnn objects, assigning weight xix_ixi​ to choosing object iii, factors as ∏i=1n(1+xi)\prod_{i=1}^n(1+x_i)∏i=1n​(1+xi​). Valiant’s question asks whether there is always some comparably economical computation. This is a question about formal polynomials over a field. The classical P versus NP question concerns decision algorithms on bit strings.

A tensor here can be pictured as a table whose entries have several indices. Joining two tables along an index means multiplying entries with matching labels and summing over that label. Matrix multiplication is already an example. In Cik=∑jAijBjkC_{ik}=\sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}Cik​=∑j​Aij​Bjk​, the two tables share jjj, while iii and kkk remain visible. A network of tables allows many such joins. Some tables simply enforce agreement, returning one when two labels match and zero otherwise.

3. Development history and previous partial results

In 1979 Valiant identified polynomials that express the full difficulty of the VNP summation. Over fields of characteristic different from two, the permanent is one of them. A small circuit for every permanent would yield small circuits for every family in VNP by substituting variables and constants. Earlier lower bounds made progress by restricting the computation. For example, Raz proved superpolynomial bounds for formulas whose intermediate polynomials are multilinear, meaning that each variable occurs to degree at most one in every monomial. The output polynomial may be multilinear even when intermediate calculations use higher powers and later cancel them. Removing restrictions on intermediate calculations and on reuse was a central difficulty. Valiant’s paper and Raz’s paper.

The algorithmic questions have benchmarks of their own. Strassen’s seven-product identity for two-by-two block matrices showed that the familiar cubic algorithm could be beaten recursively. Family 107 reported an upper exponent of 9/49/49/4 over the complex numbers. The new paper credits Amanov, van den Berg and Zuiddam with the upper bound over every field, following work of Lü. For binary integer multiplication, Harvey and van der Hoeven obtained O(nlog⁡n)O(n\log n)O(nlogn) time on tapes. The earlier family 109 reported a first saving in the power of the logarithm. Each upper bound exhibits an algorithm. To determine an exponent one also needs a reason why all faster algorithms fail in the specified model. Family 107, all-field upper bound, Harvey and van der Hoeven, and family 109.

The set problem goes back to Erdős and Rado. An rrr-petal sunflower is a collection of rrr distinct sets whose pairwise intersections are all the same set. Their classical bound guarantees a sunflower in a sufficiently large family of kkk-element sets, but includes a factorial in kkk. Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and Zhang reduced the base of the exponential to a polylogarithmic function of kkk for fixed rrr. The outstanding question was whether the base could be independent of kkk altogether. Erdős and Rado and Alweiss and coauthors.

4. What the new manuscripts claim

VP versus VNP

OpenAI’s November 23 paper claimed a superpolynomial lower bound even for circuits allowed to approach the desired coefficients in an algebraic limit. The November 24 papers give two ways to see the separation. One builds an explicit polynomial from a square grid. Label each edge, give each vertex a table, and multiply the selected table entries around the grid. Computing that product for one labeling is easy. Summing over all labelings is a VNP polynomial. The authors claim that the resulting family requires superpolynomial circuit size over every field.

Changing the graph reveals two further distinctions. On a path the state sum has a small algebraic branching program, a directed computation that sums products of edge labels along paths, but needs a large formula. On a complete binary tree it has a small circuit but needs a large branching program. Together the claims put formulas, branching programs, general circuits and VNP in a chain of four strictly increasing polynomial-size classes.

The second paper treats two familiar polynomials directly. In a directed graph with an indeterminate on every edge, the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial sums the products along tours visiting every vertex once. The permanent also permits covers consisting of several disjoint cycles. For matrices of order NNN, the claimed lower bound on circuit size is

exp⁡ ⁣(c (log⁡N)2(log⁡log⁡N)2)\exp\!\left(c\,\frac{(\log N)^2}{(\log\log N)^2}\right)exp(c(loglogN)2(logN)2​)

for an absolute constant c>0c>0c>0 and sufficiently large NNN. This eventually exceeds every fixed power of NNN. It applies to Hamiltonian cycle over every field and to the permanent outside characteristic two. In characteristic two, a minus sign equals a plus sign, so the permanent actually is the determinant. For these two polynomials the November 23 paper had obtained exp⁡(Ω((log⁡N)5/4))\exp(\Omega((\log N)^{5/4}))exp(Ω((logN)5/4)). The later bound is stronger and still allows limits of circuits that only have to get the coefficients of degree NNN right.

Sunflowers

For every fixed r≥3r\ge3r≥3 the seventh paper claims a constant BrB_rBr​ such that more than BrkB_r^kBrk​ distinct sets of size at most kkk guarantee an rrr-petal sunflower. The universe from which the sets are drawn can be arbitrarily large. For three petals, think of three committees sharing the same steering group, with every other member belonging to only one committee. The theorem says that the maximum size of a collection avoiding this pattern grows at most exponentially with the committee size.

5. Why the advance matters

Importance: 5/5. The claimed separation would exhibit an explicit polynomial whose individual contributions are easy to generate and whose sum defeats every polynomial-size arithmetic circuit. Even an algorithm designed for just one input size, with arbitrary field constants built in, falls under the bound. The matching matrix exponent would settle how far sharing calculations can reduce the cost of another basic operation. One conclusion concerns polynomial versus superpolynomial growth. The other distinguishes two powers within polynomial time. Both ask what computation must cost, a question that a faster algorithm alone can never answer.

6. The main idea

Imagine placing the ends of an equality constraint at two points in space. In the first manuscript a shared binary label has a mathematical price equal to the distance between the points. Independent constraints add their prices. A local linear change of a table cannot raise its price. These rules make the price a measuring device for networks. If a network of cheap pieces purports to produce an expensive tensor using only local operations, its pieces collectively have to pay at least the price of the output. For three sites sharing a single bit the price is half their triangle’s perimeter. For more sites it is the average projected width of their convex hull, normalized so a segment costs its length.

For the grid, place the two ends of each edge far apart along a separate axis. The coefficient table of the state sum remembers an equality on every edge. A small circuit would break that table into terms with simpler communication across cuts. Each term could be supplied cheaply across its own cut, while the output terminals stay in the same places for every term. This common placement is what lets the authors compare the price of the sum with the price of all the equalities the grid demands.

The sunflower application uses agreement in a different way. After grouping elements into kkk color classes, a set choosing one element of each color becomes a word. In a sunflower, each column of rrr such words is either constant or entirely distinct. Any partial repetition spoils the pattern. The paper constructs a tensor test that removes partial repetitions. In a sunflower-free collection of MMM words the surviving tuples consist of rrr copies of the same word. The price of sharing that choice grows as log⁡M\log MlogM, whereas the tests spend a bounded amount for each of the kkk columns. Comparing the two gives the exponential bound.

Other applications require additional arguments. Euclidean triangle prices alone give only the exponent two for matrix multiplication. The matrix paper argues that a saving below 9/49/49/4 would repeatedly compress networks for testing whether lists have repeated entries, eventually contradicting a lower bound on that test. For integer multiplication the algorithm groups binary digits into polynomial coefficients, uses short Fourier transforms to convolve them, and finally propagates carries. Sharing tape rearrangements across many short transforms is essential. A cheap Fourier gate count by itself buys no free movement of digits.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

The small term o(1)o(1)o(1) in the tape exponent tends to zero. The stated algorithm therefore allows any fixed positive slack above 1/21/21/2. Whether one machine achieves exactly O(nlog⁡n)O(n\sqrt{\log n})O(nlogn​) time remains open in the paper. The lower bound uses a weaker assumption than the usual ETH discussed in family 378. For example, an exponent v/log⁡vv/\log vv/logv saves a logarithmic factor over vvv but exceeds v1−δv^{1-\delta}v1−δ eventually for every fixed positive δ\deltaδ. This explains how a subexponential SAT algorithm and this particular conditional lower bound can coexist. Family 378.

Each exponent belongs to its own model. A scalar operation can manipulate a field element in a single gate. A tape machine must represent data, move its heads and write the answer. A border circuit describes an algebraic limit of coefficients, a different notion from numerical accuracy on floating-point inputs. The repository describes formalizations for selected statements and lists the stronger quantitative bounds that fall outside their scope. Family 379 formalization notes.
