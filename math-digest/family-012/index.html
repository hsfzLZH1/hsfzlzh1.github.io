Largest prime factors of consecutive integers: a joint Dickman law

2026-10-10

Importance 4/5 · Provisional editorial rating
Sources consulted 2026-10-10

1. The question

The integers nnn and n+1n+1n+1 share no prime factors. Does this local restriction leave a lasting connection between the sizes of their largest prime factors?

For example, sixteen has largest prime factor two, while seventeen is itself prime. Other neighboring pairs have the opposite ordering. A longstanding prediction says that each ordering should occur half the time. A stronger conjecture predicts a complete joint distribution: after taking logarithms and scaling by the logarithm of the integer, the two largest factors should behave like independent samples from the classical Dickman distribution.

Family 012 claims this stronger result in ordinary natural density. Every integer up to the cutoff is weighted equally, and the limit is asserted through every growing cutoff. The familiar half-and-half ordering is then a consequence of the joint law, rather than a separate analytic argument.

The challenge is dependence. Knowing the distribution of the largest factor of one integer does not determine the distribution of a neighboring pair, just as knowing two marginal probability distributions does not establish independence.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Dickman’s 1930 work and later results of Ramaswami and de Bruijn established the one-variable smoothness law. That marginal distribution is an input and historical starting point here, not the manuscript’s new conclusion.

Erdős and Pomerance’s 1978 paper formulated the joint problem and proved that each ordering has positive lower natural density, with an explicit bound of 0.00990.00990.0099. They also showed that the ratio of the two largest factors rarely stays too close on suitable power scales. These results revealed substantial variation without identifying a limiting distribution.

Subsequent work raised the ordering lower bounds. Results of de la Bretèche–Pomerance–Tenenbaum, Fouvry, Wang, and Lü–Wang led to the value 0.20170.20170.2017. Yang’s 2026 preprint gives 0.2800.2800.280 for both orderings. In this progression, a lower bound should not be confused with existence of a natural density or the exact value one-half.

Teräväinen’s binary-correlation theorem proved the predicted joint law in logarithmic density, and hence logarithmic density one-half for the ordering. It also gave positive lower natural density for every nondegenerate interior rectangle of normalized values. This was much more than knowing that both orderings occur often, but still involved a different averaging conclusion.

Tao and Teräväinen obtained the ordinary joint law outside an exceptional set of scales of logarithmic density zero. Their later quantitative work provides logarithmic savings and parameter uniformity while retaining exceptional scales. Other work proved conditional ordinary laws under distribution assumptions for smooth numbers, or averaged the problem over shifts.

M1 claims an unconditional ordinary limit at the fixed shift one with no exceptional scales. It does not claim to retain the quantitative error term or growing-parameter uniformity of those other results.

The reverse ordering has the same density. The deduction uses the symmetry and continuity of the limiting product distribution: it assigns equal mass to the two sides of the diagonal and zero mass to the diagonal itself. No symmetry of the finite list of consecutive pairs is assumed.

5. Why the advance matters

Provisional importance: 4/5. The claim would resolve a longstanding independence problem for consecutive integers in the original ordinary-density form. It identifies an entire joint law, of which the ordering question is one particularly accessible consequence.

The result would also demonstrate how a strong local dependence can disappear from a normalized large-factor statistic. Consecutive integers remain coprime, but their limiting largest-factor sizes would nevertheless be independent. Such statements are precise versions of probabilistic intuition in number theory.

Removing exceptional scales is mathematically meaningful. A theorem valid at almost all scales may still leave arbitrarily large cutoffs with different behavior. M1 claims to exclude every such bad sequence for fixed smoothness parameters. The rating reflects this advance and the proposed methods if correct, without reading a quantitative convergence speed into a qualitative limit.

6. The main idea

Encode the largest factor with finitely many labels

At a large scale xxx, divide primes above x1/Jx^{1/J}x1/J into finitely many logarithmic bins,

(xk/J,x(k+1)/J],1≤k<J,\bigl(x^{k/J},x^{(k+1)/J}\bigr], \qquad 1\le k<J,(xk/J,x(k+1)/J],1≤k<J,

with JJJ fixed. Counting prime factors in these bins gives a finite description of the large-factor pattern. The counts are bounded on the relevant integer range because too many large factors would make their product exceed that range.

Assign a unit complex number ζk\zeta_kζk​ to each bin and encode its count by a power of ζk\zeta_kζk​. Multiplying these powers produces a multiplicative bin function. Finite Fourier inversion recovers the probabilities of count patterns from averages of such functions, so independence reduces to decorrelation for arbitrary pairs of phase choices.

Subtract the mean from one bin function to obtain a centered function FxF_xFx​, and call the other gxg_xgx​. The required statement has the shape

The centered function is no longer multiplicative. This prevents a direct substitution into a general theorem about multiplicative functions. The manuscript instead exploits that multiplication by any fixed integer changes none of the large-prime bins once xxx is sufficiently large.

Amplify a hypothetical correlation

Assume a nonzero correlation persists along some sequence of scales. M1 introduces nonnegative divisor weights built at an auxiliary scale BBB. These weights amplify the putative bias while having controlled first and second moments.

Writing the integer through the chosen divisors permits the fixed-multiplier invariance to simplify one endpoint. Cauchy–Schwarz removes the bounded function gxg_xgx​ and yields a quadratic energy involving two copies of FxF_xFx​. After discarding a negligible diagonal, an exact change of variables converts the remaining terms into shifted products

Fx(n′) Fx(n′+j)‾.F_x(n')\,\overline{F_x(n'+j)}.Fx​(n′)Fx​(n′+j)​.

The result is a weighted graph of ordinary additive shifts. A persistent original correlation forces a positive amount of energy in this graph.

Approximate the graph by separate endpoint features

The edge weights depend on auxiliary-prime divisibility at both endpoints and on residue information. M1 compares these data with independent random prime sets, where each auxiliary prime is included with probability 1/p1/p1/p.

The goal is not to declare the actual integers independent. It is to approximate the specific graph kernel well enough for every bounded pairing that appears in the argument. The relevant cut norm measures the largest normalized matrix pairing with bounded test vectors, allowing those vectors to depend on the realized matrix.

Randomly splitting each endpoint’s prime set smooths its logarithmic and residue information. The manuscript uses this to approximate the kernel by a fixed finite sum of products of one-endpoint features. A sampling argument upgrades an integrated comparison to the needed cut-norm comparison; second-moment bounds control the potentially large kernel entries.

The manuscript establishes cancellation for short averages of FxGB,vF_xG_{B,v}Fx​GB,v​, including fixed residue restrictions, using the short-interval theorems of Matomäki–Radziwiłł and Matomäki–Radziwiłł–Tao. After the graph kernel is separated into endpoint features, its energy reduces to products of those short averages. Their vanishing contradicts the positive energy forced by the hypothetical bias.

The order of limits is part of the mechanism: the original scale xxx goes to infinity first, with auxiliary data fixed, and then BBB grows. The contradiction is applied to a subsequence of any proposed bad sequence of cutoffs, producing the claimed full ordinary limit. Refining the finite bins then recovers the largest-factor distribution.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

The theorem fixes aaa and bbb in (0,1)(0,1)(0,1). It gives no stated error rate and no uniformity when the thresholds vary with XXX. The comparison with earlier quantitative work should preserve that tradeoff: removing exceptional scales does not automatically retain stronger rates.

The displayed result concerns nnn and n+1n+1n+1. It does not state an arbitrary-length independence theorem for consecutive blocks or the entire joint factorization process. It also differs from family 011, which samples primes and studies the ranked factors of their predecessors.

The largest-factor statistic cannot encode all small-prime correlations, parity information, or coprimality constraints. Those features remain present even if this normalized joint law holds. Extensions to more shifts, sharper error bounds, and varying smoothness parameters are further questions beyond the stated conclusion.
