How narrow must a symmetric random-graph transition be

1. The question

As the edge probability of a random graph increases, a property such as connectivity changes from unlikely to likely. How wide can that transition be if the property treats every vertex label equally and is preserved when edges are added?

This question concerns the width of the transition, not where it occurs. A theorem locating the threshold near a counting prediction does not automatically determine how rapidly the probability rises through that location.

The two manuscripts claim optimal-order universal width bounds for graphs and the conjectured extension to uniform hypergraphs. M2 gives a squared logarithm in the denominator for graphs. M1 gives exponent r/(r−1)r/(r-1)r/(r−1) for rrr-uniform hypergraphs. Both are driven by stronger sensitivity estimates that hold at every edge probability, even for properties that are not increasing.

2. Background and essential definitions

In G(n,p)G(n,p)G(n,p), each possible edge among nnn vertices is present independently with probability ppp. A graph property is a yes-or-no rule unchanged by every permutation of the vertex labels. Connectivity and containing a triangle are examples.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Kahn, Kalai and Linial established a fundamental connection between Boolean Fourier analysis and influence. For a function with transitive coordinate symmetry, this forces total influence to grow logarithmically with the number of coordinates when the variance is bounded below. Bourgain, Kahn, Kalai, Katznelson and Linial extended the influence method to product probability spaces.

Friedgut and Kalai’s 1996 theorem used such estimates to prove narrow transitions for increasing properties with transitive symmetry. For graph properties, this gives a universal width of order 1/log⁡n1/\log n1/logn for fixed probability levels.

They conjectured the stronger graph bound 1/(log⁡n)21/(\log n)^21/(logn)2. Vertex relabeling supplies more information than the fact that all individual edges are equivalent: it also constrains how groups of edges overlap. Exploiting that richer action is the source of the additional logarithm.

Bourgain and Kalai approached the conjectured exponent. For every fixed η>0\eta>0η>0, they obtained graph widths of order (log⁡n)−2+η(\log n)^{-2+\eta}(logn)−2+η. For rrr-uniform hypergraphs, their influence estimates at the unbiased density p=1/2p=1/2p=1/2 approach exponent r/(r−1)r/(r-1)r/(r−1) with an arbitrary fixed loss.

Kelman, Kindler, Lifshitz, Minzer and Safra obtained, for graph properties at p=1/2p=1/2p=1/2, total influence at least a constant times

Var⁡1/2(f)(log⁡n)2(log⁡log⁡n)2.\operatorname{Var}_{1/2}(f) \frac{(\log n)^2}{(\log\log n)^2}.Var1/2​(f)(loglogn)2(logn)2​.

The present manuscripts claim to remove the remaining losses and to keep the estimates uniform throughout 0<p<10<p<10<p<1. That uniformity is needed for a general width theorem, since a property’s transition need not occur near one half.

5. Why the advance matters

Importance: 4/5. The claims settle broad quantitative sharp-threshold questions using only vertex symmetry and monotonicity, without a detailed description of the property.

For graphs, the squared-logarithm order is optimal in general at fixed probability levels. Friedgut and Kalai already noted examples involving cliques of order proportional to log⁡n\log nlogn whose widths have this scale. Some familiar properties have much narrower transitions, but a universal theorem must accommodate the worst case.

The stronger influence statement also has independent meaning. A symmetric property whose answer remains uncertain must have many opportunities for a single edge to change that answer. This conclusion survives without monotonicity, although a nonmonotone property need not have one increasing transition to which the width language applies.

The rating reflects the sharp universal graph order, the hypergraph extension and the full-density control. It is distinct from the threshold-location advances in nearby families, which compare where a transition sits with expectation-based predictions.

6. The main idea

Split variation into Fourier degrees

A function of independent edge bits has an orthogonal expansion indexed by edge subsets SSS. Under Bernoulli density ppp, one uses centered, normalized coordinate functions so that the basis remains orthonormal.

If f^(S)\widehat f(S)f​(S) denotes a coefficient, the basic identities are

The second sum weights large supports more heavily. Consequently, influence already controls the high-degree part of the variance. The problem is bounding mass on smaller edge subsets.

Restrict through vertices, preserving symmetry

Choose a random block BBB of about n\sqrt nn​ vertices. Keep every edge touching BBB free and fix all edges entirely outside it.

Every permutation within BBB still preserves the restricted function. Each free edge belongs to a large orbit under these surviving permutations, so no individual conditional influence can dominate the total. An elementary Fourier estimate then controls the restricted low-degree mass, with a bound linear in total influence.

Linearity matters because the proof averages over the fixed outside configuration and over the random block. A nonlinear loss at that stage could spoil the desired uniform estimate.

Regard a Fourier support as a small graph

An edge set SSS indexing a coefficient is itself a graph. If it has at most k2/2k^2/2k2/2 edges, it has a nonisolated vertex of degree at most kkk.

With sufficient probability, the random block meets the support only at that vertex. The restriction then retains between one and kkk edges of SSS. Thus a low-degree bound after restriction controls original Fourier supports up to a quadratic degree scale. This is where the graph’s squared logarithm enters.

For an rrr-uniform support with sss edges, the analogous combinatorial observation gives a vertex of positive degree at most rs(r−1)/rrs^{(r-1)/r}rs(r−1)/r. Inverting that relationship produces the exponent r/(r−1)r/(r-1)r/(r−1) in the original support size.

The proof tracks the factor p(1−p)\sqrt{p(1-p)}p(1−p)​ in the Fourier cutoff. In the final combination of low- and high-degree estimates, the bias dependence cancels or remains bounded. This permits densities arbitrarily close to zero or one.

Integrate sensitivity into a width bound

For increasing properties, the Margulis–Russo identity gives

q′(p)=Ip(f).q'(p)=I_p(f).q′(p)=Ip​(f).

The variance inequality therefore bounds q′(p)q'(p)q′(p) below by a multiple of q(p)(1−q(p))q(p)(1-q(p))q(p)(1−q(p)) times the relevant logarithmic power. Integrating the derivative of log⁡(q/(1−q))\log(q/(1-q))log(q/(1−q)) between the two quantiles yields the stated width estimates. This final step is why monotonicity is needed for the transition conclusion but not for the underlying sensitivity theorem.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

The symmetry is invariance under all vertex relabelings. A property singling out a distinguished vertex, edge or labeled pattern need not satisfy the same estimate. Mere transitivity of arbitrary coordinates supplies a weaker general framework.

Hypergraph uniformity rrr is fixed, and its constant may depend on rrr. The claim is not uniform when hyperedge size grows with the number of vertices.

The width bound is additive. If a threshold lies at a much smaller density than (log⁡n)−2(\log n)^{-2}(logn)−2, the universal graph estimate may say little about its relative sharpness or precise transition window. Determining exact locations, leading constants and limiting probability curves remains property-specific.

The manuscripts do not use the integral–fractional comparison or the second Kahn–Kalai theorem in families 175 and 176. Those concern threshold locations; this family’s restriction and influence argument addresses widths directly. Nor does the result settle every broader conjecture about Boolean Fourier coefficients, such as the general Fourier–entropy conjecture.

8. Sources and further reading

M2 gives the graph theorem and the density-uniform variance bound. M1, dated October 5, extends the restriction argument to hypergraphs and derives its quantile inequality.

The central historical source is Friedgut–Kalai’s sharp-threshold paper. Kelman–Kindler–Lifshitz–Minzer–Safra supplies a recent symmetry-sensitive Fourier predecessor. The manuscripts’ bibliographies identify the Kahn–Kalai–Linial influence theorem, Bourgain–Kalai’s group-symmetry refinements, and the Margulis–Russo derivative identity used in the final integration.
