1. The question

Draw a large random network on a fine square grid, and trace the boundary between an open cluster and the surrounding closed connections. The microscopic path makes innumerable right-angle turns. Does its large-scale law remember the grid, or does it become invariant under smooth angle-preserving changes of coordinates?

The Fortuin–Kasteleyn random-cluster model, usually abbreviated FK, asks this question in one family containing independent percolation, Ising connectivity, and Potts connectivity. The predicted continuum objects are Schramm–Loewner evolution, or SLE, for one distinguished interface, and conformal loop ensembles, or CLE, for collections of loops.

Family 223 claims those limits across broad ranges of the cluster parameter. It also asks two more demanding questions. Does counting lattice steps converge to the continuum curve’s natural measure? And what survives when Ising couplings acquire weak fixed randomness or the temperature moves away from criticality on the scale of the mesh?

The six manuscripts address these questions with different hypotheses. A curve’s shape, its nesting among other loops, and its occupation measure are separate conclusions.

2. Background and essential definitions

At q=1q=1q=1, the cluster factor disappears and edges are independent. At q=2q=2q=2, the model represents Ising spin connectivity.

A wired boundary arc is counted as a single connected block; a free arc has no such identifications. These bookkeeping connections must be distinguished from an actual open path using graph edges. Dobrushin boundary conditions put one wired and one free arc between two marked boundary points. A medial-lattice interface separates the primal open cluster from the dual cluster made by crossing closed primal edges.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Fortuin and Kasteleyn’s representation united several statistical-mechanical models through random connectivity. Exact solutions and Coulomb-gas calculations then predicted exponents and relations between cluster weights and continuum parameters. Schramm’s introduction of SLE turned a conformal-invariance prediction into a precise candidate law for interfaces.

Smirnov proved conformal invariance and Cardy’s crossing formula for critical triangular-lattice site percolation. FK–Ising on the square lattice became another major example through fermionic observables and the interface theorem of Chelkak, Duminil-Copin, Hongler, Kemppainen, and Smirnov. These achievements did not settle general square-lattice FK weights.

A substantial intermediate advance was rotational invariance for critical planar FK models by Duminil-Copin, Kozlowski, Krachun, Manolescu, and Oulamara. Rotation invariance is weaker than identifying the complete SLE or CLE law. M1 also acknowledges overlapping square-bond percolation claims by Tsai–Yam–Zhou and Zhou, with the latter’s stated boundary regularity and separation conditions. The present family should be read in that context.

For occupation measures, the dimension prediction was only a first step. Lawler and collaborators developed SLE natural parametrization and its identification with Minkowski content. Garban–Pete–Schramm and Holden–Li–Sun connected triangular-site percolation interface measures to that content; Lawler–Viklund did so for loop-erased random walk. These were important precedents, with different lattice models and normalizations.

Away from criticality, Makarov and Smirnov formulated the massive-SLE program. Park’s massive FK–Ising observable theorem supplied near-critical observables and crossing estimates. M4 claims the further steps needed to identify a unique interface law. Weak random bonds raise another difficulty: the Ising disorder is marginal, so microscopic randomness can decay only slowly under changes of scale.

5. Why the advance matters

Importance: 5/5. Identifying square-lattice FK interfaces throughout the claimed ranges would settle central conformal-limit predictions beyond the previously established special models. The complete nested ensemble would determine how interfaces fit together, not merely what one boundary looks like.

Natural occupation adds a quantitative geometric observable: the limiting amount of interface inside each region. The disorder and thermal results ask how robust the critical picture is and what replaces it when a macroscopic correlation scale remains.

Together these claims connect microscopic probabilities, continuum random curves, and measurable geometric quantities. Their breadth supports the highest relative rating, while the separate parameter ranges remain essential to interpreting that breadth.

6. The main idea

A central device is a boundary observable with four changes of boundary condition. Its limiting value depends on the conformal positions of four marked points. After part of an interface is revealed, conditional crossing probabilities become martingales. Two suitable tests identify the variance and drift of the limiting Loewner driver.

The difficult passage is from a formula on a fixed shape to one valid in a random slit domain. The sources develop physical crossing estimates, local comparisons, and control of narrow passages, preserving the difference between actual open paths and artificial boundary identifications. Analytic winding observables then supply the conformal information.

Below q=1q=1q=1, the usual positive-association inequality is unavailable. M2 builds restricted connection and gluing comparisons instead. At q=4q=4q=4, M6 uses a whole-plane height-field input, including the six-vertex field result discussed in family 225, and develops the additional finite-domain and conditional field arguments needed to identify interfaces.

For occupation, M5 must calibrate individual-edge visits against continuum neighborhood area. Quantitative comparisons between successive scales produce an actual amplitude, while boundary estimates prevent mass from disappearing near the square’s edges. For disorder, fixed-domain comparisons are transferred to adaptive explorations without assuming a uniform rate over every possible slit. For the massive law, energy estimates identify the drift and then support uniqueness.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

The family does not supply one theorem covering all combinations of lattice, cluster weight, disorder, and temperature. The broad critical curve theorem is square-lattice; the occupation theorem is restricted to a square and q<4q<4q<4; the below-one theorem assumes a smooth limiting boundary. Neither endpoint-uniform estimates nor a q=4q=4q=4 occupation theorem follow from these statements.

The random-bond result concerns weak symmetric two-valued disorder at q=2q=2q=2, with deterministic domain approximations. The thermal result concerns its prescribed isoradial near-critical weights and nonzero mass. It does not address arbitrary off-critical perturbations of every FK model.

Natural questions include extending occupation convergence to broader domains and parameters, treating other disorder laws, and obtaining useful rates of convergence. The manuscripts’ claims are reported as stated; the distinctions between curve, loop, and measure convergence are part of their mathematical scope.
