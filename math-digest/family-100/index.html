Covering a tetrahedron with less cylinder area than expected

2026-10-14

1. The question

Imagine covering a solid tetrahedron with infinitely long tubes. Each tube may point in its own direction, and its cross-section need not be circular. How small can the sum of the cross-sectional areas be?

The answer cannot be compared directly with the tetrahedron’s volume: area and volume have different units. A natural reference is its smallest shadow under orthogonal projection onto a plane. A familiar cover of a regular tetrahedron uses two tubes parallel to opposite edges, with total cross-sectional area exactly half that smallest shadow.

Was one half a universal lower bound? Family 100 reports that it is not. By allowing many slightly different directions, the manuscripts claim finite covers of a regular tetrahedron with cost strictly below the proposed threshold. The improvement is small, but its sign settles the proposed inequality. Two companion approximation theorems explain how continuously varying directions can be replaced by finitely many genuine cylinders without losing the saving.

3. Development history and previous partial results

The ancestor is Tarski’s plank problem. A plank is the region between two parallel planes, and its cost is their separation. Bang’s plank theorem says that the total width of planks covering a convex body is at least its minimum width. Ball later established a stronger direction-dependent plank inequality for centrally symmetric bodies.

Cylinders ask for the analogous comparison one dimension further down: a planar base replaces a one-dimensional width. The geometry changes because different axes can cooperate in ways unavailable to parallel-sided planks.

Bezdek’s Tarski’s plank problem revisited, Problem 3.1, attributes both the half-area question and the two-cylinder tetrahedral example to Bang. Bezdek and Litvak subsequently proved a directionwise lower bound of 1/31/31/3 for arbitrary three-dimensional convex bodies, and the stronger bound 111 for ellipsoids. These results show that the total relative cost cannot approach zero, while leaving a gap between the general theorem and the conjectured 1/21/21/2.

Bezdek and Khan formulated the directionwise version within their codimensional cylinder covering conjectures. The regular tetrahedron seemed especially informative: its elementary two-cylinder cover attained the proposed constant. But exhibiting equality in a simple construction does not show that the construction is optimal when the number and directions of cylinders are unrestricted.

The new manuscripts focus precisely on that remaining freedom. Their proposed counterexamples keep the tetrahedron and change the cover, exploiting a small area gain from tilting while carefully preserving overlap.

5. Why the advance matters

Provisional importance: 4/5. The family claims a counterexample to a long-standing covering conjecture and introduces finite approximation methods with uses beyond the particular tetrahedron. Its scope is more specialized than an entire theory of optimal convex-body coverings, but the conceptual change is substantial.

The regular tetrahedron’s most obvious equality construction would no longer identify the correct constant. A successful sharp inequality must account for coordinated variation of cylinder directions, rather than extrapolate from a few symmetric tubes.

There is also a useful lesson about continuous optimization. An integral may suggest a cheaper cover without actually describing a legal finite arrangement. M2 and M4 address that gap explicitly, retaining coverage while controlling the added area. The finite realization is part of the mathematical claim, not a cosmetic reformulation.

6. The main idea

Tilt saves area, but can open gaps

Start with the two-cylinder cover and split its intercept regions into small patches. Assign slightly different slopes to neighboring patches. A patch tilted relative to its cylinder’s perpendicular plane has smaller projected area: the factor is (1+∣V∣2)−1/2(1+|V|^2)^{-1/2}(1+∣V∣2)−1/2. For small slopes, the saving is quadratic.

The danger is that these tilted tubes no longer fit together. Extra padding restores coverage, but padding consumes the same area that the tilt was meant to save. The constructions arrange the geometry so that the saving exceeds this repair cost.

M1 works with narrow angular sectors. The two complementary families move their separating boundaries in opposite directions at first order. After that cancellation, a controlled second-order comparison leaves a strict gain. Fine enough sectors make discretization errors smaller still.

Fields designed around the boundary

M3 constructs a smooth rational field from a stream function that is constant on wedge walls. A stream function encodes a planar field with zero divergence; its constant boundary values also make the field tangent to those walls. These properties control both the derivative and the coverage geometry.

The complementary families’ inverse labels shift oppositely at first order. Slight enlargements then leave a second-order overlap surplus, while costing less than the projected-area saving. Further constructions use singular power fields and an angular limiting field; their behavior near the apex requires separate treatment.

These are straight segments with a direction frozen at each label, not trajectories following a differential equation. That distinction matters because a flow would generally trace curves rather than cylinder axes.

Why radial alignment helps discretization

In M4, changing the parameter changes the direction initially along the existing intercept ray. This displacement can be absorbed by moving along that ray. What remains transverse to it is only quadratic in the subinterval length.

A slightly enlarged triangle therefore contains all intercepts belonging to one subinterval. Each enlargement costs a small amount, and their total tends to zero as the partition becomes finer. M2 achieves a broader finite approximation through local affine models and coverings by square intercept tiles. The two tools have different hypotheses and geometric guarantees; neither should be read as permitting arbitrary direction fields at no cost.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

The main counterexample concerns infinite cylinders in three dimensions with freely chosen directions and measurable bases. It is not a statement about circular cylinders, bounded tube lengths, disjoint packings, or a prescribed small number of cylinders.

The manuscripts do not identify the optimal relative covering constant. Combining their stated tetrahedral counterexample with the earlier general theorem places the infimum for any nondegenerate tetrahedron in [1/3,1/2)[1/3,1/2)[1/3,1/2). Its exact value remains a separate question.

The approximation results also retain real restrictions: M2 has derivative conditions and a strict segment-length bound; M4 requires radial alignment. Their success does not justify replacing an arbitrary continuous family by finite tubes with the same integral cost.

Finally, the displayed improvement is a strict perturbative saving. It does not claim a large numerical reduction or the most economical practical cover. The importance lies in crossing the conjectured boundary and in the mechanisms that make that crossing possible.
