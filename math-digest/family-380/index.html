Fast linear programming on polytopes with long edge paths

1. The question

A workshop makes two products. Each unit of the first uses two hours on a cutting machine and one hour on a finishing machine. Each unit of the second reverses those demands. Each machine has six hours available. If both products earn one unit of profit, how much can the workshop earn? Let xxx and yyy be the quantities produced, which we allow to be real numbers. The problem is to maximize x+yx+yx+y subject to

2x+y≤6,x+2y≤6,x,y≥0.2x+y\le6,\qquad x+2y\le6,\qquad x,y\ge0.2x+y≤6,x+2y≤6,x,y≥0.

The point x=y=2x=y=2x=y=2 earns four. To see why nobody can do better, add the two machine constraints and divide by three. That gives x+y≤4x+y\le4x+y≤4. We now have a production plan and a short mathematical reason for its optimality. We could also have drawn the feasible polygon and walked around its corners to the best one.

These two approaches lead to different questions in higher dimensions. How many operations does it take to find a plan and a reason it is optimal? How many edges might one have to walk, even knowing the destination and taking the shortest route? Family 380 claims that the first question has a polynomial answer in the numbers of variables and constraints, while the second can have a superpolynomial answer in the number of facets. The distinction matters whenever a geometric picture suggests that solving a problem requires following a path through the picture.

2. Background and essential definitions

A linear program asks for the maximum of cTxc^\mathsf{T}xcTx subject to Ax≤bAx\le bAx≤b. Here xxx is a column of ddd real variables, AAA is a matrix with mmm rows, and each row describes one inequality. The feasible region is a polyhedron, the intersection of those half-spaces. A bounded polyhedron is a polytope. A facet is a face of dimension one less than the polytope, such as a side of a polygon or a square face of a cube. A vertex is a corner. An edge joins adjacent vertices.

There are two ways to measure the size of a rational linear program. One records its dimensions m,dm,dm,d. The other records the number LLL of bits needed to write every coefficient, including its numerator and denominator. Replacing one coefficient by 1+2−K1+2^{-K}1+2−K can increase LLL substantially while leaving m,dm,dm,d fixed. A bit-polynomial algorithm has running time bounded by a polynomial in LLL. A strongly polynomial algorithm uses a number of arithmetic steps bounded by a polynomial in m,dm,dm,d alone. On rational data its intermediate numbers must also have bit length polynomial in LLL. Both requirements matter. An algorithm could otherwise appear fast by manufacturing gigantic integers and charging one step for each operation on them.

For the geometry question, give every edge length one, however long it looks in a drawing. The graph diameter is the maximum, over pairs of vertices, of the number of edges in a shortest connecting path. For a ddd-dimensional cube, opposite vertices are ddd edges apart because each step changes just one coordinate. Its 2d2d2d facets give a diameter exactly equal to the number of facets minus the dimension. Cubes helped make that expression a plausible candidate for a general bound.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Dantzig’s simplex method searches by pivoting between feasible bases, typically moving between adjacent vertices. A long run for one pivot rule tells us how that rule chooses its route. To prove that every route from a given start to a given destination is long requires a lower bound on their shortest-path distance. Klee and Minty’s distorted cubes illustrated the distinction. A classical simplex rule could be made to take exponentially many pivots even though the graph retained the short paths of a cube. Klee and Minty, How good is the simplex algorithm?, cited in the LP manuscript, reference 90.

A different approach made general rational LP polynomial in bit length. In 1979 Khachiyan used separating inequalities and progressively smaller enclosing ellipsoids. In 1984 Karmarkar gave a polynomial-time interior method. Neither result supplied a bound on the general operation count solely in terms of m,dm,dm,d. In 1986 Éva Tardos showed how to remove dependence on the sizes of the objective and right-hand side for important classes of constraint matrices. This yields strongly polynomial algorithms when the matrix entries have suitably bounded encoding lengths, as happens for entries in {−1,0,1}\{-1,0,1\}{−1,0,1}. The challenge was to allow arbitrary entries in the matrix as well. Smale included the real-coefficient version as the ninth problem on his list. Khachiyan, Karmarkar, Tardos, and Smale.

On the geometric side, Hirsch had proposed in 1957 that a polyhedron with fff facets and dimension ddd should have diameter at most f−df-df−d. Santos eventually constructed a bounded example in dimension 43 with 86 facets and two vertices at least 44 steps apart. Exceeding 43 disproved the proposed bound. It still left ample room for a larger polynomial bound, perhaps even a quadratic one. Kalai and Kleitman had proved a quasipolynomial upper bound, which allows the exponent to increase with the parameters. Finding out whether one fixed power of fff would always suffice is the polynomial Hirsch problem addressed here. Santos and Kalai and Kleitman.

4. What the new manuscripts claim

An algorithm with certificates. For explicitly given real A,b,cA,b,cA,b,c, the first manuscript claims a deterministic strongly polynomial algorithm. Its output distinguishes all three possible outcomes.

  • For a finite optimum, it returns a feasible xxx and nonnegative weights yyy on the constraints such that ATy=cA^\mathsf{T}y=cATy=c and the objectives agree. For any feasible competitor zzz, multiplying its inequalities by these weights gives cTz≤bTy=cTxc^\mathsf{T}z\le b^\mathsf{T}y=c^\mathsf{T}xcTz≤bTy=cTx. In the workshop example the weights on the two machine limits are each 1/31/31/3. The paper also obtains strict complementarity. At each row, exactly one of its unused slack and its dual weight is positive.
  • For an unbounded objective, it returns a feasible point xxx and a direction rrr satisfying Ar≤0Ar\le0Ar≤0 and cTr>0c^\mathsf{T}r>0cTr>0. Every point x+trx+trx+tr for t≥0t\ge0t≥0 is feasible, and profits increase along this ray.
  • For an infeasible system, it produces nonnegative weights yyy with ATy=0A^\mathsf{T}y=0ATy=0 and bTy<0b^\mathsf{T}y<0bTy<0. Adding the inequalities with these weights would give zero at most a negative number. The paper needs at most 1+rank⁡(A)1+\operatorname{rank}(A)1+rank(A) nonzero weights for this contradiction.

Polytopes with long shortest paths. The second manuscript claims bounded simple rational polytopes with exactly fff facets at every sufficiently large fff, whose graph diameters eventually exceed fkf^kfk for every fixed kkk. “Rational” means the defining inequalities can have rational coefficients. “Simple” means that exactly ddd facets meet at each vertex in dimension ddd. The dimension grows, yet stays below every fixed positive power of fff eventually. Thus the long walks persist in a class where each corner has a particularly regular local structure.

The result even allows shortcuts. Choose a positive integer bound s(f)s(f)s(f) growing more slowly than every positive power of fff. Count a jump between any two vertices on a common face of dimension at most s(f)s(f)s(f) as one move. The authors still construct examples, depending on that choice, requiring superpolynomially many moves. They also choose an objective for which the minimum and maximum are that far apart, even on paths allowed to move in either direction in objective value.

5. Why the advance matters

Importance: 5/5. A general strongly polynomial LP algorithm would make the count of arithmetic operations depend only on how many quantities and inequalities interact. This would answer a central question that survived decades of polynomial-time LP algorithms. The companion claim explains why the same efficiency cannot be obtained merely by discovering short edge paths between arbitrary prescribed vertices. In one of the paper’s packing examples the origin and the all-ones vector are the unique worst and best vertices for the sum of the coordinates. One can write down both endpoints immediately. It is the journey along edges between them that takes so long.

The algorithm paper also claims strongly polynomial minimization for convex quadratic objectives under linear constraints. For finite turn-based mean-payoff games, two players control moves on a graph and optimize a long-run average reward. The authors give strongly polynomial time for deterministic games with perfect information and polynomial bit time for stochastic games with binary rational data. The distinction about the transition data matters even after the LP question has been answered. Earlier digest on mean-payoff and stochastic games.

6. The main idea

Finding a certificate when some entries are tiny

Write the unused amounts in the constraints as the slack vector b−Axb-Axb−Ax. The authors add a scale variable ttt and work with the linear subspace

C={(tb−Ax,t):x∈Rd, t∈R}.C=\{(tb-Ax,t):x\in\mathbb R^d,\ t\in\mathbb R\}.C={(tb−Ax,t):x∈Rd, t∈R}.

If CCC contains a nonnegative vector whose last entry is positive, divide by that entry to get a feasible point. The perpendicular subspace contains the alternatives. Indeed (y,−bTy)(y,-b^\mathsf{T}y)(y,−bTy) is perpendicular to every vector in CCC when ATy=0A^\mathsf{T}y=0ATy=0. If it is nonnegative with positive last entry, the weights yyy certify infeasibility. The feasibility question has become a search for the side that can make one distinguished coordinate positive.

Why is that difficult? Normalize the largest entry of a witness to one. Another indispensable entry might be smaller than any threshold set solely from m,dm,dm,d. The authors probe the system in different units. For a line spanned by (1/16,1)(1/16,1)(1/16,1), multiplying the first coordinate by 16 makes both entries equally visible. Scaling the perpendicular space by the reciprocal factor preserves the test for orthogonality. In a large system the algorithm has to choose the units before it knows the witness. A probe gives dependable signs for the largest rescaled entries and may give uninformative answers elsewhere.

The paper combines probes until their reliable information determines the side. It uses determinants of square submatrices to compare the magnitudes of candidate witness entries. Those ratios can be enormous even for tiny matrices. The method constructs short substitutes for the logarithms it needs, preserving the comparisons relevant to a probe. It can then ask ordinary bit-polynomial LP questions on auxiliary rational inputs whose lengths are bounded in the dimensions. Once it knows which coordinates can support a witness, it removes dispensable ones and recovers an exact certificate by linear algebra. This is how a calculation involving approximate signs is claimed to yield exact output.

Forcing a path to bend many times

The diameter paper begins with a corner in the graph of the absolute-value function. Imagine advancing an xxx-coordinate past aaa subject to z≥∣x−a∣z\ge |x-a|z≥∣x−a∣. A straight segment that touches the corner at x=ax=ax=a cannot continue through that point while satisfying both inequalities. Approaching from the left requires its slope to be at most −1-1−1, and leaving to the right requires at least 111. A path that must touch the corner must bend. Extra coordinates and tight inequalities force such contacts on paths confined to faces of small dimension. A path through the interior has more freedom.

Just inserting a fresh corner for every extra step would spend roughly as many constraints as it gained in distance. Instead the construction makes passage through each of many intervals require a whole inner crossing. An auxiliary coordinate measures progress through that crossing. Inequalities controlling different intervals reuse coordinates, keeping the growth in the facet count below the growth in forced bends. Backtracking cannot evade the count. For each crossing the authors count the part between the last visit to its near end and the first subsequent arrival at its far end. This isolates disjoint stretches on which the path has to do the work.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

The algorithm assumes explicit inequalities and exact scalar arithmetic and comparisons. It charges control work as well. With rationals, its bound on intermediate bit lengths still depends on the input bit length. Thus an instance containing million-bit coefficients still incurs the cost of manipulating them. The result gives no wall-clock comparison with a numerical solver on a factory scheduling problem. Access to a region only through an oracle that supplies separating inequalities is a different input model.

The diameter concerns the undirected graph and specified endpoints, with no monotonicity restriction on the walk. The theorem allows the dimension to grow with fff. In a fixed dimension even the number of possible vertices is polynomially bounded in fff, since a vertex can be specified by ddd independent tight constraints. The paper’s examples and the earlier quasipolynomial upper bounds therefore address a range between polynomial and unrestricted growth.

One question the algorithm paper leaves open is whether a strongly polynomial method can keep the algebraic degree of every comparison polynomial in the dimensions. Another is whether the dependence on transition-probability bit lengths can be removed for two-player stochastic games. The repository describes its Lean LP statement for rational input and its separate formalization of the diameter result. Family 380 formalization notes.
