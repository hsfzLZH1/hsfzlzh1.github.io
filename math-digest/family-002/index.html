1. The question

An elliptic curve can have infinitely many rational points even though its equation is very simple. The curve y2=x3+ax+by^2=x^3+ax+by2=x3+ax+b, when nonsingular and completed by its point at infinity, carries a group law. A basic arithmetic question asks how many independent rational points are needed to generate all the others, apart from a finite torsion subgroup.

The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, usually abbreviated BSD, predicts that this arithmetic information is encoded in an analytic function built from counting points modulo primes. It predicts both an order of vanishing and an exact leading coefficient. Knowing how many times a function vanishes does not determine the first nonzero coefficient, just as knowing that a polynomial has a double root does not determine its quadratic term.

Family 002 contains three manuscripts claiming the full formula when a prime-power Selmer group has corank zero or one. M2 supplies the unrestricted converse from this arithmetic condition to analytic rank and finiteness. M3 treats the exact power of two in the coefficient formula. M1 adds the odd-prime comparisons and assembles the exact equality.

The condition is on a Selmer group, not simply the number of independent rational points already found on the curve.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer formulated their conjectures in the 1960s from computations relating rational points to point counts over finite fields. Their Notes on elliptic curves II is a primary source for this development. Tate placed the proposed rank and leading-term identities in a wider arithmetic framework, while Cassels explained how the combined expression behaves under isogeny.

The modularity work of Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, and Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor established the analytic setting for all elliptic curves over Q\mathbb QQ. The Gross–Zagier height formula relates derivatives of suitable L-functions to heights of Heegner points. Kolyvagin’s Euler systems then yield equality of analytic and arithmetic ranks, and finiteness of Sha, when analytic rank is zero or one.

Those results run from analytic information to arithmetic conclusions. A Selmer converse reverses the direction: small arithmetic Selmer corank must force a nonzero central value or derivative. That reversal is a separate problem, particularly when the curve has difficult reduction or a reducible representation on its torsion points.

Iwasawa theory, which studies arithmetic across towers of number fields, supplied increasingly strong converse and coefficient results. Kato’s zeta elements provide one direction of comparison; Skinner–Urban, Wei Zhang, and Jetchev–Skinner–Wan obtained major low-rank results under their respective local and residual hypotheses. Later work treated additional ordinary, supersingular, Eisenstein, and multiplicative cases. These labels distinguish how the curve and its torsion behave at the chosen prime; the manuscript’s historical sections explain the precise earlier restrictions.

Complex multiplication, meaning extra endomorphisms of the elliptic curve, provides another substantial theory. Burungale–Flach establish full rank-zero BSD for their class of CM curves, with consequences over Q\mathbb QQ. Exact two-primary formulas were also known in special CM and quadratic-twist settings. The family claims to remove the remaining curve and prime restrictions under the stated low-Selmer-corank hypothesis, while retaining every numerical factor in BSD.

5. Why the advance matters

Importance: 5/5, provisional. BSD connects rational solutions, complex analysis, local arithmetic, and the failure of the local-to-global principle. An exact leading-term formula under an unrestricted low-Selmer-corank criterion would be a major advance on a central arithmetic problem.

The word “exact” has substantial content. Equality up to an unknown power of two, or up to factors at bad primes, leaves part of the conjecture unresolved. Likewise, proving that Sha is finite does not compute its order. The family claims to retain precisely the finite information that distinguishes these assertions.

Its reach is large but qualified: the initial Selmer condition remains essential. The density-one twist consequence means that exceptions have asymptotic proportion zero in the specified family; it does not eliminate every exceptional twist or prove BSD for arbitrary rank.

6. The main idea

Force analytic nonvanishing from arithmetic smallness

For the converse, M2 reduces an odd-prime problem to showing that a certain Heegner point is not torsion. Heegner points arise from elliptic curves with complex multiplication mapped into the curve under study. The appropriate height formula then connects this point to an L-value or derivative.

Assuming the point is torsion, the paper obtains two incompatible measurements of the same auxiliary extension space. The low Selmer corank bounds how many Galois extensions satisfying specified local conditions can exist. A theta-series construction, transferred through congruences of automorphic forms, produces more such extensions than that bound permits.

A theta series is a structured generating function built from arithmetic data. Here the construction retains a truncated deformation parameter, so it records successive layers of extension information rather than only isolated character values. The stated contradiction forces the Heegner point to be non-torsion. The argument uses the separate two-converse and twist-density inputs identified in the manuscript.

Measure the error prime by prime

Once rank and finiteness are available, define

Xp(E)=vp(QE)−vp ⁣(#Sha⁡(E/Q)).X_p(E)=v_p(Q_E)-v_p\!\left(\#\operatorname{Sha}(E/\mathbb Q)\right).Xp​(E)=vp​(QE​)−vp​(#Sha(E/Q)).

For odd ppp, M1 proves a nonnegative discrepancy for non-CM curves of analytic rank at most one. A second comparison pairs EEE with a suitable quadratic twist EDE^DED of complementary analytic rank and gives

Xp(E)+Xp(ED)=0.X_p(E)+X_p(E^D)=0.Xp​(E)+Xp​(ED)=0.

Two nonnegative numbers with sum zero must both vanish. CM curves are handled using the appropriate CM rank-zero input and a paired comparison. The same numerical idea appears in M3’s treatment at two, with additional machinery suited to that prime.

The difficult step is obtaining exact comparisons. The papers encode arithmetic in integral lattices and determinant lines, which track finite indices as well as vector-space dimensions. Their quotients must be units, elements with no unwanted divisibility, rather than merely integral elements. Theta comparisons and auxiliary deformation directions exclude possible extra factors. M3 treats the distinct torsion-representation cases and the real place explicitly.

Finally, M3 supplies positivity, rationality, and the correct valuation at two; M1 supplies every odd valuation. The positive rational number QE/#Sha⁡Q_E/\#\operatorname{Sha}QE​/#Sha then has exponent zero at every prime. Its prime factorization is empty, so it equals one. That last elementary observation turns the local comparisons into the full real-valued BSD formula.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

These statements concern elliptic curves over Q\mathbb QQ. They do not assert unrestricted BSD over all number fields or in higher rank. In particular, a known Mordell–Weil rank of zero or one is not substituted for the full Selmer-corank assumption.

The precise normalizations of the period, height pairing, regulator, Euler factors, and local conditions are part of the result. Changing a height by a factor of two or using a sublattice of rational points changes the numerical formula unless the corresponding correction is made.

The family does not supply an efficient general algorithm to find a basis of rational points or compute Sha. Nor does its twist-density consequence imply a bound on every exceptional curve. Higher-corank cases and broader base fields remain outside the stated coverage.
