1. The question

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm, or QAOA, alternates two simple types of quantum evolution. One encodes an objective function; the other mixes possible answers. Increasing the number of alternating layers enlarges the family of states it can prepare.

For a fixed finite instance, sufficiently many layers can approach the optimum. A harder question asks whether, for each desired accuracy, one finite depth can work for arbitrarily large random instances. The depth must be chosen before sending the system size to infinity.

Family 281 reports this property for the Gaussian zero-field Sherrington–Kirkpatrick spin glass. Its companion studies the full support of the zero-temperature Parisi order parameter, supplying one route to the classical near-optimal calculation that the QAOA construction compiles. The family concerns asymptotic attainability; it does not provide an efficient angle-selection algorithm or a quantitative depth bound.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Sherrington and Kirkpatrick introduced the model, and Parisi proposed its hierarchical order parameter. Guerra established the interpolation bound and Talagrand the positive-temperature formula. Auffinger and Chen developed the zero-temperature formula and its attainment. Determining the minimizer’s support is a separate problem from proving the formula.

Auffinger, Chen and Zeng showed infinitely many zero-temperature support points. That does not exclude an interior interval containing none. More recently, Lopatto established full interval support in the low-temperature zero-field phase, and Hong-Bin Chen established zero-temperature full support and smooth absolute continuity. M2 explicitly credits this earlier full-support conclusion. Its contribution is a different coercive-identity argument, strict positivity of the density away from zero, and scalar consequences used in optimization.

Farhi, Goldstone and Gutmann introduced QAOA. Later fixed-depth analysis of its SK performance established limiting formulas and concentration at fixed parameters. Basso, Farhi, Marwaha, Villalonga and Zhou related the limit to large-degree trees and conjectured eventual attainment of the Parisi value. Spin–boson calculations and semiclassical studies supplied further numerical and heuristic evidence.

Classical optimization supplies another key predecessor. Montanari’s Parisi-diffusion approach and the broader work of El Alaoui, Montanari and Sellke give near-optimal algorithms under support hypotheses. M1 compiles a finite classical calculation into standard QAOA controls. Its primary route uses M2; an independent positive-temperature route uses Lopatto’s support theorem and existing finite-iteration analysis.

5. Why the advance matters

Importance: 4/5, provisional. The family claims a central asymptotic attainability result for QAOA on a canonical spin glass, together with a structural analysis of the order parameter feeding the construction.

The limit order makes the result stronger than fixed-instance convergence as depth grows. One finite circuit word is chosen for an accuracy target and then used throughout the thermodynamic limit. At the same time, absent depth and angle-selection bounds prevent this from being an efficient practical optimization guarantee.

The classical near-optimal calculation also makes clear that this is not a demonstrated quantum speedup over classical algorithms. Its significance is that standard QAOA controls can asymptotically realize the required performance. The rating measures that claim if correct, while crediting the earlier support and optimization results.

6. The main idea

Exclude gaps in overlap support

M2 studies q(t)=EΦx(t,Xt)2q(t)=\mathbb E\Phi_x(t,X_t)^2q(t)=EΦx​(t,Xt​)2. Variational optimality supplies contact conditions at support points. On a hypothetical interval where the order parameter is a positive constant, an integrated rational identity gives strict convexity of q′q'q′.

The endpoint contact conditions force q′q'q′ to have average one across the interval, while strict convexity and endpoint bounds force it below one inside. This contradiction excludes an interior gap. Initial and terminal gaps require separate arguments. Once consistency holds at every time, derivative-moment identities yield smoothness and the stated positive density.

Compile a classical near-optimal formula

M1 first represents a bounded near-optimal calculation as a finite function of Gaussian variables on a rooted-tree Hilbert space. Normalized sums of child calculations supply new Gaussian coordinates. Smooth approximations and exclusions of selected vertex types prevent compiled operations from feeding back into their own controls.

Temporary longitudinal seed fields make the desired functions implementable through cost and mixer commutators. Small reserved sets of helper spins realize neighbor sums. The construction controls their contribution to energy rather than treating them as free extra resources.

Remove temporary controls before taking the limit

A further echo construction removes the longitudinal seed controls from the already selected finite circuit. Selective rotations and a large displacement produce the required effective field, while a separate energy calculation preserves the target value.

Only after all approximations and control removal does the argument choose one finite ordinary cost–mixer word and invoke the fixed-word tree-to-SK identity. This order prevents an auxiliary control or a system-size-dependent circuit from remaining hidden in the final QAOA claim.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

Both manuscripts concern the pure Gaussian zero-field SK model with the stated normalization. Nonzero external fields and general mixed-spin covariances are not included automatically.

M1 supplies existential depth and angles for limiting expected energy. It gives no explicit accuracy-depth law, efficient search procedure, finite-size guarantee or joint limit in which depth grows with nnn. Large angles may also carry implementation costs not measured by the layer count.

M2’s support result is not the first full-support statement; its distinct argument and scalar consequences should be assessed in that historical context. Quantitative QAOA bounds and extensions to other disordered optimization models remain separate questions beyond this family.
