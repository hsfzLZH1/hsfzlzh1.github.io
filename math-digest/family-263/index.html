The outer electrons of large atoms: ionization bounds and universal scales

1. The question

A nucleus of charge ZZZ attracts electrons, while the electrons repel one another. How many extra electrons can remain bound? How much energy does it take to remove one electron from a neutral atom? And how far from the nucleus are its outermost electrons?

These questions concern the edge of the electron cloud. They are surprisingly difficult even when the leading total energy of a large atom is understood. The total energy grows on a large scale, while a few outer electrons account for only a small difference between two large numbers.

Family 263 contains three manuscripts claiming uniform ionization bounds and the energy and radius parts of the generalized ionization conjecture. Their model is the full nonrelativistic Coulomb Hamiltonian with two electron spin states. In particular, the claims allow correlated many-electron wave functions, rather than restricting the state to a mean-field approximation.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Thomas and Fermi introduced a statistical approximation replacing the many-electron wave function by a density. Lieb and Simon established its variational theory and leading large-charge energy and bulk-density limits. The total energy scale is Z7/3Z^{7/3}Z7/3, and the bulk length scale is Z−1/3Z^{-1/3}Z−1/3.

Those successes do not settle ionization. If two energy formulas each have a large error, subtracting them need not accurately determine a much smaller difference. Similarly, an outer tail containing one electron represents only a fraction 1/Z1/Z1/Z of a neutral atom. Convergence of the bulk density can miss precisely that tail.

For the full quantum model, Lieb obtained charge bounds linear in nuclear charge. Lieb, Sigal, Simon and Thirring established asymptotic neutrality: the largest strictly bound electron number divided by ZZZ tends to one for large atoms. Subsequent sublinear and explicit estimates improved the excess-charge bound. But a statement such as “the excess is small compared with ZZZ” still permits an excess tending slowly to infinity.

Seco, Sigal and Solovej obtained quantitative bounds on first ionization energy and an outer-electron radius for the full model. Their estimates depended on powers of ZZZ, leaving uniform bounds distinct. M1 and M2 explain the relevant normalizations and the improved exponent recorded in that earlier paper.

Uniform ionization results were achieved earlier in approximate models, including Thomas–Fermi–von Weizsäcker theory and reduced Hartree–Fock theory. Solovej’s unrestricted Hartree–Fock theorem established bounded excess charge, bounded first ionization energy, and outer-radius control. These results are major predecessors, but their variational spaces differ from the fully correlated Schrödinger model.

Solovej’s A new look at Thomas–Fermi theory formulates generalized ionization laws using upper and lower large-ZZZ limits, followed by a large outer-electron-number limit. The current family claims the corresponding full-model conclusions, with a stronger joint limit for energy but the stated iterated limits for radii.

5. Why the advance matters

Provisional importance: 5/5. Ionization is a central test of how quantum mechanics controls matter beyond its leading bulk behavior. The claimed results describe a small exterior portion of a potentially enormous electron cloud, with constants that do not grow with nuclear charge.

The molecular bound is especially uniform: arbitrary nuclear arrangements cost only a constant number of excess electrons per nucleus. The atomic results then identify two distinct levels of information. A fixed outer tail has a bounded physical radius; a large but relatively small exterior population follows a universal Thomas–Fermi scaling law.

The full correlated model is the decisive setting. Electron correlations can alter local behavior, so results in a restricted variational model do not automatically carry over. The family claims a mechanism for recovering the relevant screening laws directly from correlated states while preserving the exact leading coefficients.

6. The main idea

The physical mechanism is screening. Electrons nearer the nucleus partly cancel its Coulomb attraction, leaving outer electrons to see a reduced effective field. A Thomas–Fermi description relates local density to the positive part of this screened field FFF:

ρ≈k(F+)3/2,k=23/23π2,\rho\approx k(F_+)^{3/2},\qquad k=\frac{2^{3/2}}{3\pi^2},ρ≈k(F+​)3/2,k=3π223/2​,

where F+=max⁡(F,0)F_+=\max(F,0)F+​=max(F,0). The challenge is to justify the appropriate comparisons for a correlated quantum state, rather than assume this relation as the model.

M1 localizes particles while recording the positions and spins removed from each region. The remaining conditional state still carries the correlations relevant to that region. Local trial-electron insertions and lower energy comparisons are arranged with matching semiclassical kinetic coefficients. A coarse kinetic inequality controls density, while the sharp coefficient comes from separate phase-space and eigenvalue-counting comparisons.

Noisy observations then produce conditional densities and screened fields. Conditioning changes probability laws and costs energy; the estimates keep those costs explicit. Positive comparison fields are propagated outward through successive spatial scales, controlling the accumulated errors. For molecules, the geometry is organized so the cost is proportional to the number of nuclei, rather than to the number of scales.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

These claims concern fixed nuclei, nonrelativistic kinetic energy, Coulomb interactions and two spin states. They do not directly describe relativistic heavy atoms, moving nuclei, magnetic fields or finite-temperature states. The universal constants depend on the stated physical normalization even when they are independent of ZZZ.

Strict binding must remain distinct from threshold-state existence. The generalized energy theorem requires both a growing number of removed electrons and a much larger nuclear charge. The radius theorem retains its iterated limit order. Neither theorem predicts individual chemical shell oscillations or fixed-mmm convergence.

The manuscripts provide universal bounds and asymptotic coefficients rather than a numerical table of ionization energies for particular elements. Effective error rates, sharper constants in the uniform bounds, and extensions to other physical Hamiltonians are separate questions. They should not be inferred from the leading laws alone.
