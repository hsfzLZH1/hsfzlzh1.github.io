All-temperature pressure for orthogonally invariant Ising spin glasses

1. The question

Knowing a symmetric matrix’s eigenvalues solves a quadratic optimization problem on the Euclidean sphere. It does not solve the same problem when every coordinate must be +1+1+1 or −1-1−1. A rotation preserves the sphere but moves the corners of the cube, so eigenvectors matter to an Ising spin system.

Suppose those eigenvectors are uniformly random. Can the limiting eigenvalue distribution then determine the system’s thermodynamics? Family 234 claims an affirmative answer at every fixed temperature, for compact limiting spectra without asymptotic outliers. It also treats deterministic external magnetic fields and derives zero-temperature optimization limits.

The resulting answer is a variational formula: a precisely defined optimization over possible hierarchies of spin overlaps. It need not reduce to an elementary expression, but it turns an exponentially large random sum into a deterministic mathematical problem.

2. Background and essential definitions

Random interactions and pressure

There are NNN spins, assembled into a vector σ∈{−1,1}N\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^Nσ∈{−1,1}N. Write the interaction matrix as

JN=UNTΛNUN,ΛN=diag⁡(λ1,N,…,λN,N).J_N=U_N^{\mathsf T}\Lambda_NU_N, \qquad \Lambda_N=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1,N},\ldots,\lambda_{N,N}).JN​=UNT​ΛN​UN​,ΛN​=diag(λ1,N​,…,λN,N​).

The empirical spectral measure

records the fraction of eigenvalues in each region; δx\delta_xδx​ denotes a point mass. Its limit is μ\muμ, a probability law supported in a compact interval. The smallest and largest eigenvalues must approach the corresponding endpoints of the support. Weak convergence of μN\mu_NμN​ alone is insufficient, because it can overlook a small number of extreme eigenvalues.

Overlaps and hierarchies

Two independent equilibrium samples at the same interaction matrix are called replicas. Their overlap is

Q(σ,τ)=1N∑i=1Nσiτi.Q(\sigma,\tau)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\sigma_i\tau_i.Q(σ,τ)=N1​i=1∑N​σi​τi​.

It is 111 for identical configurations, −1-1−1 for opposites, and near zero for largely unrelated signs. In a spin glass, several levels of similarity can survive as NNN grows.

The formula uses a nondecreasing function p:(0,1)→[0,1]p:(0,1)\to[0,1]p:(0,1)→[0,1], interpreted as a quantile description of a trial overlap hierarchy. The nonnegative convention comes from small auxiliary perturbations used in the analysis; it does not mean unperturbed finite systems have no negative overlaps.

3. Development history and previous partial results

The Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model introduced a mean-field spin glass with Gaussian pair interactions. Parisi proposed that its low-temperature behavior requires a hierarchy of overlaps rather than one scalar similarity. Guerra proved the variational upper bound, and Talagrand established the matching pressure formula. Aizenman, Sims and Starr developed a complementary cavity formulation based on adding spins. SK model, Guerra’s bound, Talagrand’s formula, cavity principle.

Orthogonally invariant models ask how much of that picture survives with more general spectra. Statistical-mechanics calculations for the random orthogonal model and related interactions developed spectral and replica-symmetry-breaking predictions. These provided important motivation, but are different from an all-temperature theorem under explicit matrix assumptions. Marinari, Parisi and Ritort, Cherrier, Dean and Lefèvre.

Rigorous results had reached substantial high-temperature regimes. Bhattacharya and Sen treated zero-field pressure. Fan and Wu obtained a replica-symmetric formula with external fields at sufficiently high temperature. The manuscript also credits 2026 work by Fan, Misiakiewicz, Wang and Wen giving an explicit high-temperature criterion for a constant field through dynamical mean-field analysis. Zero-field result, external-field result, 2026 development.

A further ingredient comes from random-matrix theory: Guionnet and Maïda analyzed spherical integrals using the spectral R-transform, including behavior at a spectral edge. A spherical integral averages over continuously varying directions. The quenched Ising pressure averages the logarithm of a sum over cube vertices, so this earlier theorem does not by itself answer the present question. Spherical-integral theorem.

The claimed advance is to remove the high-temperature restriction while retaining the full overlap hierarchy and a general limiting spectrum.

5. Why the advance matters

Importance: 4/5. If correct, an all-temperature formula for this broad matrix class substantially extends the thermodynamic theory beyond high-temperature regimes. It links spin-glass organization to spectral data while preserving the possibility of complicated overlap hierarchies.

The result also reaches Gaussian-pattern Hopfield interactions. If GNG_NGN​ is an ⌊αN⌋\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor⌊αN⌋-by-NNN matrix of independent standard Gaussian entries, then

JN=cNGNTGNJ_N=\frac cN G_N^{\mathsf T}G_NJN​=Nc​GNT​GN​

is covered for every fixed real ccc. Positive and negative signs, followed by zero-temperature limits, determine respectively the largest and smallest lengths of signed sums of the Gaussian columns, with the manuscript’s normalizations. Thus the same thermodynamic formula has consequences for discrete quadratic optimization and Gaussian operator norms.

The score reflects the breadth of this extension, rather than suggesting the variational problem is automatically easy to compute or that an efficient optimization algorithm follows.

6. The main idea

The argument begins with a spectrum taking finitely many distinct values. Rotate equilibrium configurations into spectral coordinates and measure their overlaps separately inside each eigenspace. Their sum is the ordinary spin overlap.

Small perturbations enforce identities relating several replicas. Established ultrametricity and synchronization theory then reduce these many overlap arrays to compatible nondecreasing functions. Rotation identities connect the eigenspace functions to a single total-overlap function. This is the step that makes the eigenvalue law enter through the spectral transform.

For one pressure bound, add hierarchical Gaussian fields to a trial system and vary the interaction strength. The comparison is arranged to keep the supremum over fields inside the infimum over trial overlap functions. Preserving this optimization order is central to the stated formula.

For the other bound, couple systems with slightly different numbers of spins. Random directions within eigenspaces generate approximately Gaussian fields for the added spins. Evaluating this cavity increment produces the same single-spin and spectral terms. A concavity argument identifies the required field supremum.

Finally, approximate general compact spectra by finite alphabets and use concentration on the orthogonal group to turn mean convergence into almost-sure convergence. External fields require an additional constrained-magnetization argument. The common theme is to separate the randomness of directions, the spectral information and the hierarchy of spin similarities without discarding their interactions.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

The absence of asymptotic spectral outliers is an assumption. A vanishing fraction of eigenvalues can be invisible to the empirical law yet strongly affect the cube’s energy. The manuscript also gives an example showing that weak convergence to an unbounded-support law is not enough for the conclusion.

For random spectra, the conditional eigenvector law must be Haar. Spectral convergence in probability gives pressure convergence in probability; almost-sure spectral hypotheses give almost-sure pressure convergence. Convergence of expectations additionally requires uniform integrability. A random external field dependent on the matrix requires the corresponding Haar condition after conditioning on both spectrum and field data.

The pressure includes diagonal self-interactions. Removing them subtracts half the limiting mean eigenvalue. Physical free energy at inverse temperature β>0\beta>0β>0 is minus the chosen pressure divided by β\betaβ, with the counting-prior correction included if appropriate.

An exact variational characterization need not identify its optimizer explicitly. Determining phase boundaries, fluctuation laws, computational complexity and concrete minimizing hierarchies are further questions. The thermodynamic limit is taken before the zero-temperature limit in the ground-state consequence; the manuscript does not require a uniform all-temperature finite-size estimate to obtain it.
