Two-point Chowla: do neighboring integers forget prime-factor parity?

1. The question

Count the prime factors of a positive integer, including repetitions, and record whether that count is even or odd. For example, 12=22⋅312=2^2\cdot312=22⋅3 has three prime factors and 10=2⋅510=2\cdot510=2⋅5 has two. Does the parity for nnn give any persistent information about the parity for n+1n+1n+1?

The two-point Chowla conjecture predicts that the two parities become uncorrelated when averaged over longer and longer initial intervals. The same question can be asked about nnn and n+hn+hn+h for any fixed positive shift hhh, or about two nonproportional linear expressions in nnn.

Family 007’s manuscript claims ordinary two-point cancellation at every sufficiently large scale, with a power-of-logarithm saving for the Liouville function. It also claims a broader qualitative theorem for bounded multiplicative functions that do not imitate a periodic character times a smooth multiplicative oscillation.

The word “ordinary” carries much of the significance. Each integer receives equal weight. Earlier theorems established logarithmically weighted cancellation, averages over shifts, or conclusions outside exceptional sets of scales. Those are major partial results, but they do not by themselves give the asserted every-cutoff conclusion.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Chowla proposed a general vanishing principle for products of Liouville values at distinct fixed shifts. The binary case is already difficult because multiplicativity controls products of arguments, while the question compares additive shifts.

Elliott extended the problem to correlations of bounded multiplicative functions. Work of Matomäki, Radziwiłł, and Tao identified a necessary correction for complex functions: a function can resemble different twists at successive scales without resembling one fixed twist globally. Their averaged form of Chowla’s conjecture explains the corrected condition and proves cancellation averaged over ranges of shifts.

Matomäki and Radziwiłł’s short-interval theorem showed that most short averages of a bounded real multiplicative function approximate its long average. They also obtained a strict improvement over the trivial fixed-shift correlation bound, without showing the normalized correlation tends to zero at every cutoff.

Tao’s logarithmically averaged theorem established the binary corrected Elliott conclusion with logarithmic weights, including nonproportional affine forms. Helfgott and Radziwiłł then developed divisibility graphs and quantitative expansion estimates. Pilatte’s work improved the saving for the logarithmically weighted two-point Liouville correlation to a power of a logarithm.

For ordinary averages, Tao and Teräväinen obtained binary cancellation outside a set of scales of logarithmic density zero. Later work of Klurman, Mangerel, and Teräväinen allowed a weaker nonpretentiousness assumption along a suitable set of scales. M1 claims to remove exceptional cutoffs under the uniform condition stated above, and to give a quantitative Liouville bound for fixed affine forms.

The manuscript explicitly builds on these graph and short-interval methods. Its claimed advance lies in the finite-scale estimates and transfers needed for ordinary averages, rather than in introducing multiplicative correlations from scratch.

5. Why the advance matters

Provisional importance: 5/5. Ordinary two-point Chowla is a central test of the principle that multiplicative arithmetic can behave randomly under additive shifts. An every-cutoff theorem would remove a persistent gap between several strong averaged results and the original correlation question.

The affine and general-function formulations broaden the scope beyond neighboring integers. They describe when multiplicative structure can survive in a shifted correlation and when absence of a specific kind of structure forces cancellation.

The quantitative distinction also matters: a fixed logarithmic saving provides a definite asymptotic bound for Liouville, while the general theorem guarantees convergence with no common speed. The rating concerns the significance of these stated conclusions if correct, rather than the apparent randomness of numerical experiments.

6. The main idea

Build a graph from multiplication and divisibility

Multiplicativity relates the values at n,n+hn,n+hn,n+h to values at dn,d(n+h)dn,d(n+h)dn,d(n+h). The latter arguments differ by hdhdhd. The manuscript organizes such comparisons as a weighted graph on integers, with steps determined by products of selected primes.

For a prime ppp, the indicator 1p∣n\mathbf1_{p\mid n}1p∣n​ has average approximately 1/p1/p1/p. Subtracting that mean produces the centered quantity

1p∣n−1p.\mathbf1_{p\mid n}-\frac1p.1p∣n​−p1​.

Centering makes cancellation available when a prime is averaged independently. Short-interval Fourier estimates control the error introduced when the original divisibility weights are replaced by these signed expressions.

A graph is encoded by a matrix. Large powers of that matrix count closed walks, and bounds for their traces can control the size of the associated operator. In arithmetic graphs, repeated prime labels create congruence constraints. Walks with many independent constraints are rare. When fewer constraints remain, the manuscript organizes the repetition patterns with forests, keeping the number of possible patterns under control.

Obtain a saving that survives at the final scale

For Liouville, each graph step takes one prime from each of JJJ disjoint supplies. The total useful weight grows roughly like WJW^JWJ, whereas the graph bound has size roughly (CW)J(C\sqrt W)^J(CW​)J, with a large fixed WWW. Choosing WWW sufficiently large gives exponential decay in JJJ.

The construction takes JJJ proportional to log⁡log⁡X\log\log XloglogX. Exponential decay in JJJ then becomes a negative power of log⁡X\log XlogX, explaining the form of the headline saving. Extra disjoint prime factors control how divisor weight is distributed among short multiplicative intervals.

A finite interval of integers is too short to average over the full joint period of all selected primes. M1 instead compares the specific residue tests used in the argument with an independent-residue model. It invokes bounded-independence results from Boolean circuit theory after correcting the encoded distribution to exact bounded independence. This step is what allows the comparison to remain quantitative at the actual cutoff.

Why the general theorem uses a different limit

Uniform nonpretentiousness may diverge arbitrarily slowly. The general-function argument therefore fixes an auxiliary graph scale BBB, lets the ordinary averaging interval grow, and only afterward lets BBB increase.

A hypothetical persistent correlation would force a graph average of size comparable to B−1B^{-1}B−1. The analytic replacement error is smaller, while the graph estimate gains an additional fixed negative power of BBB. Increasing BBB contradicts the original bias.

The weights here differ from the Liouville construction. Certain signed divisibility factors are not individually mean zero; the needed cancellation occurs only after including normalization factors from both endpoints of a walk. M1 keeps those prime residues unconditioned until the signed average is taken. Taking absolute values too early would lose the effect the strategy relies on.

These are two related graph arguments with different quantitative information, not one rate estimate automatically valid for every multiplicative function.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

The manuscript addresses two-point correlations. It does not settle Chowla’s full conjecture for every number of distinct shifts. Pairwise cancellation does not imply cancellation of every longer product.

All shifts, affine coefficients, and residue moduli in the conclusions are fixed. The every-cutoff assertion should not be interpreted as a uniform theorem for parameters increasing with the cutoff. The positive logarithmic exponent is absolute, but the implied constants may be ineffective.

The general corrected Elliott statement needs uniform nonpretentiousness over a growing twist range. A hypothesis excluding only fixed twists would not be the theorem stated here. Neither the Möbius specialization nor arbitrary bounded multiplicative functions inherit a quantitative rate from the Liouville result.

Natural further questions concern higher correlations, stronger savings, and simultaneous growth of the shifts or moduli. They remain beyond this family’s stated conclusions.
