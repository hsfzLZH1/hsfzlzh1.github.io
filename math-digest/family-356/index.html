Recognizing Alexandrov curvature without a smooth metric

2026-10-26

1. The question

On a smooth curved space, curvature can be described in two very different ways. One differentiates the metric and computes a curvature tensor. The other draws geodesic triangles and compares them with triangles on a sphere, plane or hyperbolic plane.

These descriptions agree in classical Riemannian geometry. But a singular space may have distances and shortest paths without possessing a smooth metric tensor in coordinates. Can a weak, distributional curvature tensor still capture exactly the geometric triangle condition?

Family 356 claims a positive answer to Gigli’s characterization conjecture in every integer dimension at least two. Its first manuscript identifies Alexandrov lower curvature with a lower bound for Gigli’s distributional sectional curvature, within an appropriate synthetic Ricci-curvature framework and with the exact Hausdorff reference measure.

A second manuscript supplies an essential bridge: under precise boundedness assumptions, a distributional Hessian inequality controls a function along every minimizing geodesic. This is stronger than an almost-everywhere assertion, because one particular geodesic may lie entirely inside a set of measure zero.

2. Background and essential definitions

Triangles as curvature detectors

A geodesic is a path realizing the distance between its endpoints, parametrized at constant speed. An Alexandrov space with curvature at least κ\kappaκ, abbreviated CBB(κ)\mathrm{CBB}(\kappa)CBB(κ), satisfies lower triangle comparison with the simply connected two-dimensional model of constant curvature κ\kappaκ.

Informally, its triangles are at least as “fat” as the corresponding model triangles: appropriate distances between points on their sides are no smaller than in the comparison triangle. When κ>0\kappa>0κ>0, the comparison is restricted to the usual model scale. This is a distance-based condition, meaningful even at singular points.

On a smooth manifold, sectional curvature assigns curvature to each two-dimensional tangent plane. If X,YX,YX,Y span that plane, the denominator in its formula is

∣X∣2∣Y∣2−⟨X,Y⟩2,|X|^2|Y|^2-\langle X,Y\rangle^2,∣X∣2∣Y∣2−⟨X,Y⟩2,

the squared area of their parallelogram. The numerator is a component of the Riemann curvature tensor. Ricci curvature sums appropriate sectional curvatures; thus sectional curvature at least κ\kappaκ implies Ricci curvature at least (n−1)κ(n-1)\kappa(n−1)κ in dimension nnn.

The RCD framework

A metric measure space (M,d,m)(M,d,m)(M,d,m) combines a distance ddd with a reference measure mmm. Full support means every nonempty open ball has positive measure. The measure is assumed finite on bounded sets.

The condition RCD(K,N)\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)RCD(K,N) is a synthetic version of a Ricci lower bound KKK and a dimension upper bound NNN. Its curvature-dimension part describes convexity of suitable entropy quantities when probability distributions are moved by optimal transport. “Riemannian” adds that the energy of a function’s gradient is quadratic, so the weak first-order calculus behaves like a Hilbert space rather than a general normed geometry.

This framework permits weak gradients, Laplacians, Hessians and covariant derivatives without assuming a smooth atlas. The manuscripts use the unreduced curvature-dimension convention.

The measure Hn\mathcal H^nHn is nnn-dimensional Hausdorff measure, defined from coverings by small sets and their diameters. On a smooth nnn-manifold it is the usual geometric volume, with the standard normalization. Requiring m=Hnm=\mathcal H^nm=Hn is an exact structural assumption, not just a statement that the measures have the same null sets.

What “distributional” means

A distributional derivative is defined through integration by parts against suitable test functions. It can exist when pointwise derivatives do not.

Gigli’s curvature functional R(X,Y,Z,W)(f)\mathcal R(X,Y,Z,W)(f)R(X,Y,Z,W)(f) similarly pairs a weak curvature expression with a scalar test function fff. In the smooth case it equals the integral of f⟨R(X,Y)Z,W⟩f\langle R(X,Y)Z,W\ranglef⟨R(X,Y)Z,W⟩. In the nonsmooth case integration by parts expresses it using available weak first covariant derivatives, avoiding an assumed classical curvature tensor.

The allowed global test functions are bounded and globally Lipschitz, belong to the L2L^2L2-Laplacian domain, and have Laplacian in W1,2W^{1,2}W1,2. Here W1,2W^{1,2}W1,2 means square-integrable functions with square-integrable weak gradient. Test vector fields are finite sums ∑jaj∇bj\sum_j a_j\nabla b_j∑j​aj​∇bj​ with aj,bja_j,b_jaj​,bj​ in that test class. These domain conditions matter on noncompact spaces.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Lott–Villani and Sturm developed synthetic Ricci lower bounds using optimal transport. Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré introduced the RCD condition to select a Riemannian subclass, and subsequent work developed its local and second-order analysis.

On the geometric side, Petrunin and Zhang–Zhu connected Alexandrov lower curvature with synthetic Ricci bounds. The Sobolev theory of Kuwae, Machigashira and Shioya supplied the quadratic energy structure. Thus Alexandrov spaces were already known to fit into the analytic framework relevant here.

Gigli’s 2019 paper defined the distributional Riemann tensor on RCD spaces and proposed the characterization at issue. Defining a tensor with the expected algebraic symmetries was not enough to show that its lower bound controls metric triangles.

Several partial connections clarified the remaining obstacles. Ketterer and Mondino characterized sectional and intermediate Ricci bounds by transport in the smooth setting. Lebedeva and Petrunin constructed a weak curvature tensor for Alexandrov spaces admitting suitable noncollapsing smooth approximations. That is a different framework from a characterization using Gigli’s original global test classes on an arbitrary space satisfying the stated assumptions.

Brena and Gigli studied measure representations of weak Hessians and curvature tensors and identified regularity issues in turning a weak Hessian bound into geodesic convexity. Work on C1C^1C1 Riemannian metrics also obtained distributional-to-Alexandrov comparison in that more regular setting.

The companion manuscript builds on earlier changes-of-measure and entropy arguments of Ketterer, Sturm and Han. Its claimed contribution is to justify the required analytic steps under the weaker distributional Hessian hypothesis and to recover the bound on each prescribed geodesic, using Deng’s nonbranching theory.

4. What the new manuscripts claim

M1: an exact characterization

Let n≥2n\ge2n≥2 be an integer, let κ∈R\kappa\in\mathbb Rκ∈R, and let (M,d)(M,d)(M,d) be complete and separable. Completeness means that Cauchy sequences converge; separability means there is a countable dense subset.

M1 claims that the following are equivalent:

  • (M,d)(M,d)(M,d) is an nnn-dimensional Alexandrov space with curvature at least κ\kappaκ.
  • With m=Hnm=\mathcal H^nm=Hn, it is a full-support RCD((n−1)κ,n)\mathrm{RCD}((n-1)\kappa,n)RCD((n−1)κ,n) space and satisfies
R(X,Y,Y,X)(f)≥κ∫Mf(∣X∣2∣Y∣2−⟨X,Y⟩2) dm\mathcal R(X,Y,Y,X)(f) \ge \kappa\int_M f\bigl(|X|^2|Y|^2-\langle X,Y\rangle^2\bigr)\,dmR(X,Y,Y,X)(f)≥κ∫M​f(∣X∣2∣Y∣2−⟨X,Y⟩2)dm

for all global test vector fields X,YX,YX,Y and all nonnegative global test functions fff described above.

In the smooth case this is precisely the integrated sectional-curvature lower bound. In the claimed theorem it includes the singular geometry through the weak functional. It is not merely a curvature inequality on a smooth regular subset, nor a theorem restricted to compactly supported test fields.

5. Why the advance matters

Importance: 5/5, conditional on the claims. M1 would identify two fundamental languages of curvature on singular spaces: triangle comparison and distributional tensor calculus. It is an equivalence under the stated hypotheses, providing both a geometric interpretation of the weak tensor bound and an analytic characterization of Alexandrov geometry.

The companion theorem addresses a recurring difficulty in analysis on nonsmooth spaces. An integral inequality sees sets through a measure, while geometry asks questions about individual paths. Moving from almost-everywhere information to every geodesic is therefore a substantive step, not a change of wording.

Together the papers would allow weak differential estimates to recover geometric comparison without first smoothing the entire space. Their scope remains tied to the RCD and measure assumptions; the claim is not that an arbitrary metric space carries a usable curvature tensor.

6. The main idea

Extract curvature from small metric configurations

For the Alexandrov-to-tensor direction, M1 compares small configurations of points and integrates the comparison against transported densities. In smooth geometry, curvature first appears in a higher-order expansion of distances. The manuscript develops an integrated version that uses only the weak derivatives available in the test calculus.

Averaging cancels lower-order terms. The remaining coefficient is the desired curvature functional plus a nonnegative square error. Local choices of gradient tests make that error small; approximation and exhaustion then restore the original global test classes. Controlling changes in the transported densities is necessary because the Hessian coefficients are only square-integrable.

Recover a second-variation inequality from the tensor bound

For the converse, heat flow regularizes transport between densities. A forward heat factor and a suitably normalized backward factor yield a continuity equation describing the movement of mass. Their logarithmic potentials produce a weak version of a Riccati calculation, the matrix differential relation behind classical curvature comparison.

After controlling acceleration and singular measure terms, the zero-viscosity limit yields an index inequality. In smooth geometry an index form measures how the energy of a geodesic changes under transverse variations. Here the inequality is obtained through the weak calculus and weighted transport estimates.

To use it, the paper constructs frames—collections of independent tangent directions—along relevant paths. Spatial almost-everywhere regularity would not automatically provide a frame at every time. The argument combines noncollapsed structure, estimates for exceptional sets, Deng’s continuity of tangent cones, and a countable collection of trial fields. These trials play the role of classical Jacobi variations and yield a radial Hessian comparison.

Change measure to reach an individual path

M2 supplies the next step. For λ>0\lambda>0λ>0, change the reference measure to

mλ=eλFm.m_\lambda=e^{\lambda F}m.mλ​=eλFm.

The proposed weighted Bochner inequality has curvature bound K−λGK-\lambda GK−λG. A Bochner inequality relates the Laplacian of gradient energy to second derivatives and curvature. Its weighted form translates the Hessian information about FFF into an entropy inequality for the new measure.

The delicate point is operator domains. A bounded Lipschitz weight preserves the relevant Laplacian generator domain, but need not preserve the stronger test-function class. The manuscript establishes the needed multiplier regularity and closes the inequality in a common graph domain before applying curvature-dimension results. It does not assume a formal change-of-measure calculation is automatically legitimate.

For a probability measure μ\muμ, the entropy identity is

Ent⁡mλ(μ)=Ent⁡m(μ)−λ∫F dμ.\operatorname{Ent}_{m_\lambda}(\mu) =\operatorname{Ent}_m(\mu)-\lambda\int F\,d\mu.Entmλ​​(μ)=Entm​(μ)−λ∫Fdμ.

Thus, after dividing by λ\lambdaλ, an entropy inequality can become an inequality for averages of FFF. The proof concentrates endpoint measures near a strict interior subsegment of a chosen geodesic. Nonbranching makes that subsegment the unique minimizing connection between its endpoints, forcing the concentrating transports to recover it. Taking λ\lambdaλ sufficiently large removes the residual entropy terms.

This yields the second-derivative inequality along the chosen path, including paths an almost-everywhere statement might miss.

Return from functions to triangles

M1 applies M2 to bounded global modifications of distance functions. These satisfy the required weak Hessian bounds and produce the appropriate one-dimensional model inequality along every geodesic. Ordinary differential comparison then gives local triangle comparison, and the Alexandrov local-to-global theorem supplies the global curvature bound.

M2 is an independent analytic input in this chain. Its proof does not assume the characterization that M1 is trying to establish.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

M1 requires integer dimension n≥2n\ge2n≥2, the exact measure Hn\mathcal H^nHn, full support and the stated unreduced RCD bound. Replacing that measure by an arbitrary positive density, dropping the Ricci framework, or restricting curvature tests to a convenient regular subset changes the assertion.

M2 has broader measure scope but specific function hypotheses: bounded globally Lipschitz FFF, bounded continuous GGG, and finite synthetic dimension greater than one. Its conclusion is distributional along paths; it does not make FFF twice classically differentiable.

Neither result says every RCD space is Alexandrov. The extra distributional sectional bound is decisive. Nor does the characterization provide a smooth atlas or a pointwise classical tensor at every singular point.

Extensions to weaker function bounds, other measures and different dimensional settings are separate questions. The mathematical arguments and equivalences are reported as stated, without proof or novelty verification.

8. Sources and further reading

M1 states the characterization and identifies precisely where M2 is used. M2 states the independent weak-Hessian theorem and explains its change-of-measure regularity issue.

Useful primary sources are Gigli’s original tensor and conjecture, Brena–Gigli on fine Hessian representations, Deng on tangent cones and nonbranching, and De Philippis–Gigli on noncollapsed RCD spaces.
