1. The question

Scatter points randomly across the plane. Give each point the region closer to it than to any other point, and color these regions independently black or white. Can a connected black path cross a prescribed rectangle or a more complicated shape?

This is Poisson–Voronoi percolation. Unlike a square or triangular lattice, its geometry is itself random, with cells of many shapes and sizes. Yet its large-scale critical behavior is expected to agree with that of familiar planar lattice models.

Family 224 asks three increasingly detailed questions. At the critical coloring probability, is a crossing probability given by Cardy’s conformal formula? If black becomes slightly more likely, do the thresholds at which many crossings appear have the same joint limit as on the triangular lattice? Finally, can the precise width of that transition window be written as a constant times a power of the microscopic scale?

The three manuscripts form a dependency chain. M3 claims the critical crossing formula. M1 takes that formula as an explicit input and claims a joint near-critical universality theorem. M2 uses the same input, together with results from M1, to determine the pivotal normalization more sharply.

An annealed probability averages over both points and colors. A quenched probability first fixes the point configuration and averages only the colors. Consequently, a quenched crossing probability is a random number depending on the tessellation. Showing that this number becomes nearly deterministic is an additional statement beyond computing its average.

A quadrilateral, or quad, is a bounded Jordan domain with four distinct boundary marks a,b,c,da,b,c,da,b,c,d in cyclic order. Its sides can be curved. A crossing between arcs [a,b][a,b][a,b] and [c,d][c,d][c,d] means a connected subset of the black cells, clipped to the closed domain, meeting both arcs. The cells come from the full-plane tessellation. Cells centered outside the domain can participate, and boundary points are not artificially wired together.

3. Development history and previous partial results

Cardy’s conformal-field-theory calculation predicted a crossing probability determined only by the conformal shape of a marked domain. Smirnov established this formula for critical site percolation on the triangular lattice. Subsequent SLE and loop-limit theory supplied a detailed continuum description there. Smirnov’s original crossing paper is the foundational rigorous example.

Voronoi percolation has exact rotational symmetry in distribution, but that alone does not determine its crossing probabilities. Bollobás and Riordan established its critical probability of one half. Tassion proved scale-uniform box-crossing estimates. These estimates keep crossing probabilities away from zero and one for fixed rectangle shapes; they do not identify their exact limits.

Ahlberg, Griffiths, Morris, and Tassion showed concentration of quenched crossing probabilities and developed quenched noise sensitivity. Vanneuville’s annealed scaling relations and quantitative quenched arm estimates supplied further control of rare connections and shared random geometry. The new crossing claim builds on this probabilistic foundation.

The near-critical mechanism was clarified by Kesten’s scaling relations: a macroscopic crossing changes when one of its rare pivotal sites changes color. Garban, Pete, and Schramm constructed triangular-lattice near-critical and dynamical scaling limits using continuum pivotal measures. M1 uses that model as its reference, with an explicit normalization through the mean pivotal count.

There is also a distinction between exponents and amplitudes. An exponent predicts a power only up to smaller logarithmic-scale errors. A limiting amplitude gives a fixed positive multiplicative constant. Sharp triangular-lattice arm asymptotics were already known in work of Du, Gao, Li, and Zhuang; M2 concerns a specific Voronoi pivotal observable under its stated crossing input.

5. Why the advance matters

Importance: 5/5. The family targets a central universality question for a planar model with genuinely random geometry. The claimed crossing theorem would identify its conformal law, while the companion results would transfer substantially richer near-critical information from the triangular lattice.

The quenched conclusion is especially informative: a typical fine tessellation would have the same limiting threshold law after averaging only colors. The randomness of the cells would cease to create a separate macroscopic law.

The amplitude makes the scale explicit. Doubling spatial resolution would shrink the density window by the corresponding 3/43/43/4 power, with a fixed model-dependent conversion factor. That factor need not match the triangular model’s factor; universality appears after each model is normalized in its own units.

The importance rating applies to this combination of claims as stated. The conditional structure of the companion theorems remains part of the result, rather than being hidden by the shared family label.

6. The main idea

M3 must recover an analytic observable from irregular geometry. On the dual triangulation, local triangles can be compared with equilateral ones by affine maps. Their displacement fields split into a conformal part and a geometric defect. Exact identities around closed cluster boundaries control these quantities, but an interface calculation requires control along open pieces.

The manuscript describes a transport construction that closes those pieces using prescribed local configurations and connecting corridors. Comparing different closures isolates endpoint contributions and a common bulk term. The resulting cancellation identifies a holomorphic limit, whose boundary values determine a conformal map to an equilateral triangle and hence Cardy’s function. This addresses the irregular embedding rather than treating rotational symmetry as a substitute for conformal invariance.

M1 then extracts joint critical information by exploring colors on a fixed geometry and applying crossing tests in the unexplored regions. To change density, it expands the effect of changing finitely many color histories. Alternating-arm estimates control the contributions of multiple nearby pivotal locations.

A local comparison replaces a microscopic color insertion by a small-square modification. It retains the signs of the resulting differences, which ordinary absolute-probability estimates could lose. Two independent color replicas on the same tessellation finally control variation over geometry and yield the quenched conclusion.

M2 sharpens the scale by studying transmission of connection information across long annuli. When four interfaces survive, the relevant difference has one dominant component. For a symmetric signed insertion, that component cancels, giving faster decay for distant geometric changes. Integrating these effects controls the derivative with respect to Poisson intensity and produces a summable error around the predicted exponent. A qualitative Cardy limit is enough to start the multiscale contraction; no rate in that input is assumed.

7. Scope, limitations, and open questions

The critical theorem averages geometry and colors in arbitrary fixed Jordan quads. The near-critical theorem is a stronger conditional-law statement, but is formulated for the specified countable rational polygonal family and monotone coloring coupling. It is not automatically a theorem in every possible topology on time-dependent interfaces.

The tessellation stays fixed as ppp varies. This differs from moving the Poisson points or refreshing colors dynamically. Earlier dynamical-percolation work motivates the methods, but these main conclusions concern monotone thresholds.

The pivotal amplitude is annealed and model-specific. It does not assert that every sampled tessellation has exactly that pivotal count, nor that an arbitrary random planar tessellation shares the same constant. All statements concern the planar homogeneous Poisson model with independent coloring.

Questions beyond the displayed claims include quantitative rates, other random geometries, and stronger path-space descriptions of near-critical interfaces. Extending such conclusions requires additional arguments; scalar crossing convergence alone is not interchangeable with a full coupled continuum model.
